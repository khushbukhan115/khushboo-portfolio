Somvique Lux DTC e-commerce

From badge-led to product-led premium

Redesigning a high-consideration DTC purchase decision around product evidence, fit, transaction clarity and recoverability.

A premium mattress product page was carrying recognition, promotion and product messaging, but the shopper still had to resolve basic purchase questions about fit, configuration, payable price, delivery and recovery. I reframed the problem from visual luxury into purchase-decision architecture.

01The business problem

Selling something customers cannot try through the screen

Somvique is a premium direct-to-consumer mattress brand moving further into the luxury segment. Lux is the flagship expression of that position, so its product page has two jobs: show why Lux deserves a premium position, and help someone decide whether to buy it.

The second job is the hard one. Through a screen, a shopper cannot lie on the mattress, feel its firmness or judge its scale. They cannot tell what “medium-plush” means for their body, how it works for two sleepers, or whether a claim applies to them.

Looking more luxurious could not solve that. The page had to replace part of what a showroom provides.

The commercial tension

The commercial tension was just as real. Strip out commerce for a calmer look and the transaction gets harder to understand; add promotion, urgency and cross-sell and premium collapses into discounting. A sale that returns because of fit is not necessarily a success, so conversion quality mattered as much as conversion. The system also had to extend across a five-product range and, later, to mobile.

02Evidence

The deeper problem was uncertainty

I investigated the purchase from six directions: the category’s shift from showrooms to DTC and back towards omnichannel, owned and external reviews, independent testing, a seven-brand study of premium strategies, sixteen cross-category benchmarks and published usability and CRO research. Four decision modes and nine extremes became evaluation lenses, not marketing characters.

The evidence converged: shoppers were not short of persuasion. They were carrying seven kinds of uncertainty at once.

How the evidence changed decisions

  1. Competitor landscape
    FindingPremium has no universal visual formula. It is strongest when attached to something the product can credibly own, and traceable proof beats badge volume.
    →
    DecisionI stopped treating luxury minimalism as the strategy and made product substance carry the premium.
  2. CRO and usability
    FindingShoppers look for delivery information before Add to Cart. A documented external experiment tied truthful delivery information to a 27.1% revenue lift.
    →
    DecisionI moved truthful delivery confidence next to the commitment, without manufactured urgency. I took the mechanism, not the number.
  3. Customer evidence
    FindingFit is conditional, and independent testing suggested some claims were more conditional than the brand story implied.
    →
    DecisionI made fit a product responsibility: explain its boundaries instead of offering universal reassurance.
  4. Cross-category systems
    FindingStrong configurable products keep every dependent value synchronized and state the physical consequence of each choice.
    →
    DecisionI made one authoritative purchase state the core of the architecture.

03The turning point

The page was solving the wrong decisions first

The obvious reading was that the page was too dense and not luxurious enough. The evidence pointed elsewhere. Awards, scale claims, cooling language, promotion and a competing upgrade filled the first fold. The payable price and the primary Add to Cart sat below it, and delivery and recovery sat further down still.

  1. AssumedA visual-density problem
  2. EvidenceResearch across six streams
  3. DiagnosedA decision-sequence problem
  4. ResponseA new purchase architecture

The first fold was not primarily too dense. It was resolving the wrong decisions first.

Why the diagnosis mattered

A density diagnosis would have produced a visual-cleanup project. A sequencing diagnosis required a new purchase architecture.

From that point, every element had to earn its place by the decision it helps the shopper resolve.

04Product thesis

Three rules replaced the redesign brief

I turned the diagnosis into three operating rules and judged every concept, module and claim against them.

The third rule changed the status of policy content. In this category, delivery, trial, returns and warranty are not footer policies: they compensate for buying an expensive physical product sight unseen, so they belong next to the commitment.

A filter for exploration

The rules also gave exploration a filter. A concept could look premium and still fail by breaking the sequence, or look efficient and still fail by weakening product truth or recovery.

05Exploration

Real content broke the obvious layouts

I explored five materially different architectures. I rejected two early for weak information logic, not weaker looks, and built the other three as working HTML with identical content and controls so they could be compared structurally.

Placeholder content made several structures look viable. With real configuration, price, delivery, fit and recovery content, every finalist exceeded the decision surface in at least one commercial state. The question became what must be visible now, what only needs to be reachable now, and what belongs later.

What the measurement showed

Information architecture was a larger lever than visual compression.

The permanently expanded size grid was one of the largest sources of overflow, so the final design makes the selected size unmistakable and keeps all nine configurations reachable without forcing them into the first layer.

An honest change of direction

The strongest structural concept did not become the final composition; a later paper exploration produced the two-column direction. The structural work had already locked the sequence, decision budget, content tiers and stress conditions that direction had to pass.

06Purchase system

The PDP became a purchase system

The final first fold has two coordinated responsibilities. The left side carries product evidence; the right side resolves one purchase in decision order. Underneath both sits a single purchase state, so neither side can contradict the other.

This is where premium and conversion stop competing. The shopper who doubts scale or feel gets evidence instead of another beauty shot, and the shopper ready to buy never has to reconcile one size with another size’s price.

Configuration is transaction state, not UI selection

Selecting a configuration updates every dependent value from one record. All nine configurations are represented explicitly rather than derived from neighbouring values, because commerce errors often start when separate modules compute or cache their own numbers.

Split King is the proof. It is not another width: it ships as two mattresses, so the scale view, dimension line, price, gift logic and cart outcome must change together. The system treats it as a different purchase state, not a different button label.

Five decisions that protect the purchase

  1. 01Product-led premium

    Before

    Recognition and broad claims carried much of the premium argument.

    Decision

    Physical profile, construction, material, scale and fit evidence carry it instead.

  2. 02Commercial truth

    Before

    Competing commercial messages left the shopper to work out the real price.

    Decision

    Payable price leads. A comparison value appears as Total Value where it applies, not automatically turned into a “Save X” claim.

  3. 03Delivery truth

    Before

    Urgency could overwhelm operational meaning.

    Decision

    An estimated range, an editable location and the real condition that can move it. The countdown is gone.

  4. 04Fit boundaries

    Before

    Comfort language implied the mattress suits everyone.

    Decision

    Medium-plush is translated into consequences, with fit boundaries disclosed progressively. A high Add to Cart rate followed by a fit-driven return is not a better outcome.

  5. 05Protect the primary decision

    Before

    A competing mattress upgrade sat inside the core Lux decision.

    Decision

    Removed from the primary path. Resolve this purchase first, then test whether an upsell earns its added complexity.

07AI as a controlled production system

AI accelerated production. It did not get authority over product truth.

I used AI for breadth and speed: environmental scenes, variation, exploration, research synthesis, critique and documentation. It never authored what a shopper relies on as fact: the mattress, its logo and quilting, verified construction, pricing, commercial data, product claims or unverified engineering geometry.

I prepared seven product cutouts across six angles from verified sources, so each generation began close to the requested camera view and only the environment was generated. Every output then passed a fidelity gate.

When a model got it wrong

Reject, do not repair product truth.

When a model changed the product, I never retouched the fake. I changed the source, the camera, the model or the approach. Running the same prompts across tools showed each was strongest at protecting a different kind of truth, so tool choice became a routing decision.

AI increased production capability without becoming the decision-maker.

08Validation

Validation was designed to find faults, not confirm the concept

I layered four kinds of pressure test because each catches a different class of failure. The aim was a process capable of disproving the design.

08Outcome

A purchase that is easier to understand, easier to justify and built to scale.

The redesign changes what a shopper has to work out before paying, and what the business has to rebuild for every new mattress. Each theme below pairs what changed in the design with the commercial effect it is built to produce.

01Decision quality

The transaction resolves before commitment

Configuration, dimensions, price, financing, promotion, gift, delivery and cart resolve together, with price, delivery and recovery placed where transaction anxiety is highest. That is designed to cut ambiguity, configuration errors and reconciliation work, supporting purchase confidence and checkout continuation with fewer price, configuration and delivery questions.

What changed in the design

  • Payable price and the primary Add to Cart moved from below the first fold into the decision surface
  • Nine configurations resolve through one authoritative purchase state
  • Delivery, trial, returns and warranty sit beside the commitment

02Product value

The premium price becomes explainable

Construction, materials, profile, fit and scale give the shopper reasons to accept the price without leaning on discounts or badges. Qualifying fit before Add to Cart is meant to reduce purchases that fail later because the mattress was never right: a stronger conversion model than optimizing Add to Cart alone.

What changed in the design

  • Recognition badges and broad claims gave way to inspectable product evidence
  • Four media states, each answering a different purchase question
  • Medium-plush translated into consequences, with fit boundaries one step away

03System quality

One decision model extends across the range

Invariants are separated from product variables, so the same model can extend to four more mattresses and mobile without redesigning the commerce logic each time. The handoff defines what a designer decides alone, re-verifies or escalates. That is product-system leverage, not a one-off page.

What changed in the design

  • 76–150px recovered by re-weighting content, against 18–54px from spacing reduction
  • Four accessibility defects found and fixed in rendered QA
  • A rollout order across the range, starting with the closest neighbouring product

Business impact to validate

The measures I would use after launch.

The model is simple: less ambiguity before commitment should improve the quality of the purchase decision before it moves any single top-line metric.

Decision comprehension

  • Time to resolved price
  • Configuration backtracking

Progression

  • PDP to Add to Cart
  • Checkout continuation

Purchase quality

  • Fit-related return reasons
  • Delivery support contacts
  • Price and promotion support contacts

Business guardrails

  • Revenue per session
  • Upgrade mix

A higher Add to Cart rate paired with more cancellations or fit-related returns is not a better outcome.

Somvique was not a visual luxury redesign. It was a purchase-decision redesign.
