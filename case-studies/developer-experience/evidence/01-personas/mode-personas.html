The three modes a developer occupies on this portal

Rather than profile types of developers (junior versus senior, language background, role), this document profiles the three modes a developer passes through on the portal. Each mode produces different requirements, surfaces different design tensions, and stress-tests a different strategic decision in the redesign.

Why these three personas

Each persona was chosen to stress-test one of the three strategic decisions the redesign has to take a clear position on. A single developer cycles through all three modes — sometimes within a week, sometimes within an hour. Designing for these three together guarantees the redesign serves the full lifecycle.

Context & Background

Maya is 35, a Senior Backend Engineer / Tech Lead at a 200-person B2B SaaS company in EMEA. Her VP Eng has asked her to evaluate three candidate APIs for a critical integration and recommend one. She has twelve years of experience and has integrated 30+ APIs across her career. She is currently not writing code — three competitor portals are open in adjacent tabs, a Notion comparison matrix is taking shape, and her API spec viewer of choice is ready to load each vendor's OpenAPI file.

Goals

She wants to decide whether to commit to this vendor, understand the API's full capability surface (not just the happy path), identify gaps between the marketing pitch and the technical reality, and anticipate the integration's hidden costs — rate limits, idiomatic SDK quality, edge-case behaviour, deprecation history.

Needs

A HTTP-first reference that does not force her into an SDK she may never use. A capability map that lets her scan what the API can and cannot do, organised by job rather than by resource. Comprehensive error documentation (status codes, semantics, recovery patterns). Pricing, rate limits, SLA, and deprecation policy in the portal — not buried on the marketing site. Code samples she can read for quality, not just paste.

Pain Points & Frustrations

SDK-only portals that hide the HTTP layer — she cannot tell whether the API is sound or whether the SDK is wrapping smelly code. Walkthroughs that feel like marketing demos instead of reference material. Auto-generated docs that show parameter names but not semantics or constraints. Missing error response shapes — the success body is shown, failures get a status code and silence. AI assistants that hallucinate references or summarise away the precision she is looking for.

Behaviours & Habits

Opens five-plus tabs from the portal in the first thirty seconds. Searches for specific keywords — "rate limit", "OAuth", "webhook", "error", "idempotency". Reads the Concepts and changelog before the endpoint reference. Tests her hypotheses by reading the API spec directly when the docs are vague. Uses ctrl-F more than the portal's own search. Bookmarks specific pages to share with her team and to revisit during procurement.

Psychographics

Motivations
Deliver a sound integration; avoid 18 months of "we picked the wrong API"; defend the choice with evidence.
Fears
Hidden costs; vendor lock-in; support quality after the sales call; AI-summarised docs that hide caveats.
Attitudes
Skeptical by default. Treats vendor docs as marketing until proven otherwise.
Beliefs
"Good APIs have ugly truths in their docs." "The SDK is a convenience, not the contract."
Risk Tolerance
Low–medium. Would rather skip a feature than be surprised by it.
Trust Orientation
Trusts precision and disclosure. Distrusts polish that hides detail.
Decision Style
Comparison-driven, evidence-led, defends the choice in writing.
Technical Confidence
Very high. Comfortable at the spec level.

Decision Criteria

Can she find the HTTP-level truth without fighting the docs? Does the API have a coherent error model? Are SDKs in her team's languages idiomatic or autogenerated noise? Is there a changelog and a deprecation policy she can audit? What is the failure-recovery story for the operations her product depends on?

Journey Touchpoints on the Beacon Portal

Lands on Overview, wants "what this API does," not "welcome to the docs." Scans the endpoint list — finds it organised by resource (Albums, Artists), not by capability (search, playback). Searches "error", gets few results. Switches to HTTP — finds it, but the page is still framed as "X SDK in HTTP," revealing the SDK-first IA. Looks for a Pricing / Limits / SLA page — not present in the portal. Tries the Copilot — useful but does not cite specific endpoints; loses confidence.

Key Scenario

Maya has three tabs open — Beacon's portal, Stainless's portal, a competitor's docs. She is making a recommendation by end of week. She searches "rate limit" in each. The portal that returns a clear, structured, cited answer wins the engineering eval.

Product / UX Implications

The portal must elevate HTTP-first as a co-equal lens, not as one of seven languages buried in a switcher. A capability-first navigation cut, in parallel with the resource-first cut, would let her scan the API by job. Errors, rate limits, and operational constraints need their own first-class surfaces — an error catalogue, recovery patterns, retry semantics on every endpoint. The Copilot must cite specific docs and link to them; answers without citation cost trust. Concepts must surface as part of the reference, not buried under Quick Start as if they were onboarding artefacts.

Context & Background

Diego is 28, a full-stack developer at a Series A startup of forty people. Five years of experience. This API is one feature inside a larger product release. He is the only engineer assigned to it and the PM wants it live in four working days. He works in VS Code, with a terminal, Postman, and a browser tab on the portal. His team uses TypeScript on the front and Python on the back; he picks the language based on which side of the stack the API call lives on.

Goals

Ship a working integration by Friday. Avoid spending more time on auth than on the actual logic. Use the official SDK because his team does not trust raw HTTP in production. Get something working locally, then harden it.

Needs

A working code sample in his language that he can paste and run. A fast path through OAuth setup, or guidance on what credentials to get and where to get them. A playground that returns a real response so he knows the shape before writing code. Recipes and walkthroughs for the most common two or three integration patterns. An AI assistant he can ask for "give me this in TypeScript with retry logic."

Pain Points & Frustrations

"It works in the docs but not in my code" — sample omits an import or a config step. Auth pages that explain OAuth conceptually but do not tell him where to click in the provider's dashboard. Walkthroughs that show the happy path but not how to wire it into a real app. Docs that explain the function signature but not when to call it. Copilot answers that do not include the imports.

Behaviours & Habits

Skims, does not read — looks for code blocks first and prose second. Copies the first example that looks close and tweaks it. Opens the playground, clicks "Try It!", uses the response to plan his types. Asks the AI assistant before reading the reference. Mostly working in his language of choice; rarely switches to other languages.

Psychographics

Motivations
Ship on time; look reliable to his PM; learn enough to defend his choice in code review.
Fears
Hitting a wall with auth on Day 2 and burning his timeline. AI suggesting code that does not work with no debug path.
Attitudes
Pragmatic. "I will read the spec only if the example fails."
Beliefs
"The SDK is doing the right thing." "The error message is enough if it is specific."
Risk Tolerance
Medium. Uses AI-generated code if it has working tests. Copies-tweaks confidently.
Trust Orientation
Trusts working examples; trusts SDKs more than raw HTTP; trusts AI with citations.
Decision Style
Fast, experimentation-driven, "try it and see."
Technical Confidence
High in his stack. Medium-low in OAuth and infrastructure.

Decision Criteria

Did the example just work? Did the Copilot give him code that compiled on the first try? Is the auth setup under thirty minutes? Are error messages precise enough to fix on his own?

Journey Touchpoints

Lands on Quick Start, skips to the code blocks. Goes straight to the endpoint via left nav, not search. Clicks Try It! before reading the spec. Hits OAuth, gets stuck, opens Copilot or navigates to Concepts → Apps. If Copilot unblocks him, he becomes a power user of it for the rest of the integration. Returns later for a different endpoint — uses search if he remembers the name, navigates to the resource if he does not.

Key Scenario

Friday morning. Diego has 4 hours before the PR review. He clicks "Try It!" on the Get-an-Album endpoint, hits the OAuth wall, asks the Copilot for help. The Copilot's next answer decides whether he ships today or rolls into next sprint.

Product / UX Implications

The "Try It!" playground must work out of the box with safe demo credentials, OR the auth setup must be the smoothest path in the product — linked inline from every endpoint page that requires auth, with step-by-step guidance for getting credentials from the provider's dashboard. The Copilot must produce complete, runnable code with imports, instantiation, and the call — not just the call. Walkthroughs are valuable for him because they tie multiple endpoints into a real use case, but only if they map to actual product patterns, not toy demos. Errors in his SDK call should map to specific docs sections in one click.

Context & Background

Sara is 32, a mid-level backend engineer at a fifty-person product team. She maintains six active API integrations including this one. She did not write the original integration — she inherited it from a teammate who left. Right now she is dealing with a 502 error in production; PagerDuty fired twenty minutes ago. She has Datadog dashboards open in another window, the codebase in her editor, and the API portal in a third tab.

Goals

Stop the bleeding — production is degraded and customers are noticing. Identify whether the error is on her side, the API's side, or a transient infrastructure issue. Find the right retry / backoff / circuit-breaker semantics. Know whether the API has had a recent change that could explain the regression.

Needs

Search that returns specific error codes and their meanings. A clear status / changelog / breaking-changes page. Idempotency, retry, and rate-limit documentation that is prominent, not buried. Response examples for every status code — not just 200. A way to filter docs by "things that can break" rather than wading through capability docs.

Pain Points & Frustrations

Search that returns marketing pages when she searches "500" or "429". Error pages that say "Internal Server Error" with no playbook. Changelogs that are press-release-style instead of API-developer-facing. Walkthroughs that explain the happy path but never the recovery. Discovering that yes-the-API-changed-last-week buried in a blog post, not the portal.

Behaviours & Habits

Uses portal search first, ctrl-F second, navigation third. Reads only the error tabs (401, 403, 429, 500); skips the success body. Always checks the changelog page before assuming her code is wrong. Opens the Copilot only as a last resort because "AI cannot tell me what just broke." Bookmarks the error-code reference if one exists.

Psychographics

Motivations
Restore service; document the postmortem; never see this error again.
Fears
Being blamed for an outage that is the API's fault. Missing a breaking-change announcement. Introducing a fix that masks the real problem.
Attitudes
Distrust until proved otherwise. Treats the portal as evidence in an investigation, not as a teacher.
Beliefs
"The portal lies about edge cases." "The status page is more honest than the docs."
Risk Tolerance
Very low. If she cannot verify a behaviour, she will not depend on it.
Trust Orientation
Trusts precision, timestamps, explicit "this changed on X date" notes. Distrusts blanket statements.
Decision Style
Investigative, evidence-led, conservative.
Technical Confidence
Very high in production debugging. Agnostic about SDK ergonomics.

Decision Criteria

Did the portal tell her what 502 means in this API's vocabulary? Is there a changelog with the actual change? Are retry semantics specified, or does she have to infer them? Can she tell from the error response alone whether this is a transient or a permanent failure?

Journey Touchpoints

Lands via Google or a bookmark, not the home page. Searches "502", "rate limit", "retry-after", or the failing endpoint. Goes to the endpoint page, clicks the 5xx response tab — finds nothing useful. Looks for changelog or status — does not find it in the portal. Falls back to Copilot, asks "what does a 502 from this endpoint mean?" — gets a probabilistic answer that does not help her root-cause. Navigates away to Stack Overflow or X to find the truth.

Key Scenario

Product / UX Implications

An Error Catalogue must be a first-class IA branch — parallel to Endpoints and Models. Every error code documented: when it fires, what the response body looks like, how to recover, idempotency guidance. A Changelog must be prominent in the portal nav with API-developer framing, not marketing release notes. Search must weight error codes, status codes, and concept terms heavily, not bury them. The Copilot must know when changes were made and which endpoints they affected — it must be wired into the changelog as a knowledge source. Per-endpoint "what can break" surface, summarising rate limits, retry behaviour, idempotency, and recent changes.

Cross-persona insights

What all three personas have in common

All three trust precision over polish. All three are made nervous by AI assistants that summarise away detail. All three reach for search before navigation when the question is specific. All three care about errors more than the marketing site implies.

Where they diverge

Maya wants breadth and HTTP-first truth. Diego wants a working SDK example in his language. Sara wants error semantics and changelogs. A portal that serves only one will fail the other two. The redesign needs to layer the surfaces so each persona can find their preferred entry point without the others' content getting in the way.

What this means for the IA

The current portal organises around content type (Quick Start, Walkthroughs, Endpoints, Models). The persona analysis suggests a parallel organising principle is needed: mode of use. A developer in Maya-mode wants to scan capability and risk. Diego-mode wants to do. Sara-mode wants to fix. The redesign should support all three without requiring the user to declare which mode they are in — the surfaces should be self-evidently useful to each.

What this means for the AI Copilot

The Copilot's contract changes per persona:

  • For Maya: cite sources, link to the spec, surface limits and edge cases proactively.
  • For Diego: produce complete, runnable code with imports, instantiation, and the call. Be the friction-relief on OAuth setup.
  • For Sara: know the changelog. Cite which version of the API the answer applies to. Refuse to guess when the question is about state ("what just changed?").

A Copilot that does only one of these well is a Copilot that disappoints two of the three personas.

Validation plan

The personas above are designer-led syntheses, not research-validated artefacts. To harden them, the following research is recommended before finalising the redesign direction.

  • Five to eight semi-structured interviews with developers who have recently used an Beacon-built portal. Mixed-mode — some in evaluation, some in integration, some in maintenance.
  • Search log analysis from a representative Beacon-customer portal. Validates Sara's behaviour (do error terms appear?) and Maya's behaviour (do capability terms appear?).
  • Time-to-first-successful-call analytics segmented by language and by entry page. Validates Diego's OAuth-friction-cliff hypothesis.
  • Heatmap analysis of the Responses section on endpoint pages. Quantifies how often non-200 tabs are clicked.
  • AI Copilot transcript review (anonymised, with consent). Classifies question types — evaluator, integrator, maintainer — and assesses answer quality against each.
