This document adds five personas placed along a single dimension — developer expertise and API sophistication — and forces the redesign to defend itself at both extremes.

Why design for extremes

The technique comes from inclusive design (Patricia Moore's foundational work; Microsoft's Inclusive Design Toolkit; the BBC Gel system). The core argument is simple:

If you design for the average user, you fail the extremes. If you design for the extremes, you often pull the average up too.

For Beacon specifically, this matters for three reasons:

  1. 2026 reality. AI agents and no-code/low-code platforms (Zapier, Make, n8n, Retool, plus the new wave of agentic builders) mean non-engineers and adjacent roles are increasingly landing on developer portals. Designing only for "developers" is too narrow. Postman's 2025 State of the API Report found 89% of developers using generative AI daily and 51% of organisations deploying AI agents; both populations spill onto API portals.
  2. Strategic moat. Beacon's competitive position rests on being the integrated portal experience — better than Stainless or Speakeasy as a whole DX surface, not just as an SDK generator. That moat shows up at the edges: a portal that an architect respects AND a portal a no-code builder can navigate is one nobody else delivers.
  3. Internal consistency. A portal that serves only the middle implicitly punishes the edges — junior developers get lost, architects get patronised. The design is invisibly hostile to both.

Context

Aria is 31, a Marketing Operations Manager at a 60-person B2B SaaS company. She has spent three years building automations in Zapier and Make to connect HubSpot, Slack, Notion, Asana, and the company's product database. She has never written code professionally — she considers herself "not technical" even though she configures complex multi-step automations daily.

Today her Resonance-to-Slack integration broke. Zapier's connector reports "Resonance returned 401" and points her to "check the API documentation." She has the docs open, Zapier open, and the company's developer Slack channel open in case she needs to escalate.

Goals & Needs

She wants to get the workflow running again, ideally before standup. She needs to know whether this is a "click somewhere in settings" problem or a "needs a developer" problem. She needs plain-English explanations, step-by-step recovery instructions that do not assume CLI access, and a way to tell the difference between "my problem" and "the API's problem."

Pain Points

SDK-only documentation that assumes she will write code. Error messages like "OAuth token expired" without explaining how to refresh one. Jargon (rate limit, idempotency, retry-after, scope) treated as common knowledge. Code blocks she cannot read or modify. "Try It!" buttons that demand credentials she does not have access to obtain.

Behaviours

Reads the prose, skips the code blocks entirely. Uses ctrl-F for plain-English error text more than for status codes. Asks the AI assistant in conversational language ("why isn't this working?"). Falls back to the no-code platform's community forums first, then internal Slack.

Psychographics

  • Motivations Keep the workflow running. Look competent. Avoid escalating to engineering.
  • Fears Breaking something further. Admitting she is out of her depth. Being told "this is a developer question."
  • Attitudes APIs are mysterious infrastructure. Documentation is intimidating. AI is a friend who explains things kindly.
  • Risk Tolerance Very low. Will not click anything labelled "regenerate" or "revoke."
  • Trust Orientation Trusts plain language and visual guidance. Distrusts terminal commands.
  • Technical Confidence Low. Three years of Zapier; she considers it "not real" technical.

Decision Criteria

Can she identify and fix the problem without escalating? Are the recovery steps clickable, not codeable? Did the AI explain the error in language she understands? Is there a way to tell whether the issue is hers, the no-code platform's, or the API's?

Key Scenario

Tuesday standup is in 40 minutes. Aria's Zapier flow has been broken for 12 hours. She opens the API docs, sees a wall of code, asks the Copilot "why am I getting 401?" If the answer leads with curl -H Authorization:, she escalates. If it explains the token expired in plain English, she fixes it herself.

⚡ Why this extreme matters

By 2027 most organisations will have Aria-equivalents running automations on the same APIs developers consume (Gartner: 65% low-code; Postman 2025: 51% of orgs have deployed AI agents). A portal that fails Aria fails the next 5–10 years of API consumption. Beacon's integrated portal-plus-SDK-plus-AI is the rare position that can serve both audiences from one surface — pretending otherwise is a strategic blind spot.

Product / UX Implications

  • A "By-task" access path alongside "by-SDK" and "by-endpoint" — entry points framed around outcomes like "Why is my integration suddenly returning 401?", "How do I refresh my access token?", "Where do I find my Client ID?"
  • Plain-English error explanations prepended to every status-code page; technical detail below the fold.
  • An AI Copilot mode that detects non-developer context and adjusts vocabulary accordingly — when the user asks "why isn't this working?", the response should not lead with a code sample.
  • Decision-tree guides for common recovery flows: "401 received → is this you or the API? Try this. Still failing? Here's how to escalate."
  • Screenshots and visual references for the provider's dashboard, not just the API itself.
Evidence & Confidence Medium · Designer-synthesised
  • Postman 2025 State of the API: 89% of developers use generative AI daily; growth of API consumption by non-traditional users
  • Gartner forecast: 65% of application development will use low-code by 2027
  • Stack Overflow 2024: learners trust AI more than professionals (49% vs 42%) — the same pattern likely extends to citizen integrators
  • Postman 2025: 51% of organisations have deployed AI agents — Aria interacts with portals indirectly via these agents

The citizen-integrator persona is well-documented in no-code platform UX research and in the broader RevOps / MarOps literature. Their specific interaction with raw API portals — as opposed to abstracted no-code connectors — is less directly studied. Worth treating as a hypothesis worth honouring: the trend lines (AI agents + low-code growth) suggest this population will only grow.

Context

Liam is 24, a Junior Full-Stack Developer at a thirty-person startup. He is six months into his first developer job, having come from a twelve-week bootcamp. He is strong on JavaScript/React and learning Node and APIs. His first real integration: connecting the product to a music API for a feature. His tech lead said "use their docs and the SDK." Stack Overflow, ChatGPT, and the docs are all open simultaneously.

Goals & Needs

He wants to complete the integration without asking too many questions in Slack. He needs to learn enough about OAuth to defend his choices in code review. He needs walkthroughs that explain why, not just what. He needs code samples that include imports, environment variable setup, and deployment notes. He needs concept pages that connect "what is a scope?" to "how does that show up in the code?"

Pain Points

Code samples that omit the import statement. OAuth tutorials that explain the flow conceptually but not how to register the app in the provider's dashboard. Errors like invalid_scope with no link to the scopes documentation. AI suggestions that include three different libraries when he only knows one. "Setting up SDKs" pages that assume he already knows how to manage env vars and secrets.

Behaviours

Copies examples wholesale, then deletes lines until something breaks to learn what each does. Asks ChatGPT before reading the docs. Reads tutorials linearly. Does not search the portal — navigates by clicking. Bookmarks pages he did not fully understand to revisit.

Psychographics

  • Motivations Ship the thing. Learn the craft. Look competent at standup.
  • Fears Asking a "stupid question" in Slack. Pushing code that gets shredded in review. OAuth becoming a multi-day yak shave.
  • Attitudes Documentation is a teacher first, reference second. AI is a study buddy.
  • Risk Tolerance Medium. Will paste AI-generated code if it compiles.
  • Trust Orientation Trusts step-by-step tutorials, AI that explains its reasoning, and senior teammates.
  • Technical Confidence Low-medium. Strong in his bootcamp languages, fragile elsewhere.

Decision Criteria

Did the tutorial explain why, not just what? Did the example include everything needed to run it? Did the AI explain the error in beginner-friendly language without dumbing it down? Did the concept pages link back to the endpoints that use them?

Key Scenario

First day implementing the integration. Liam reads Quick Start, copies the OAuth flow code, doesn't fully understand it but pastes anyway. The endpoint returns 401. He asks the Copilot "why?" The Copilot either teaches him about scopes — or hands him a working snippet he ships without understanding. The design choice teaches him a habit he keeps for years.

⚡ Why this extreme matters

If the portal fails Liam, it fails the next generation of integrators. Liam grows up into Diego in three to five years; if his first integration with this API was a frustrating slog, his future team will choose competitors. Junior developers are also the population most exposed to AI dependency — they will absorb whatever interaction patterns the Copilot teaches them. The Copilot's design choices propagate up.

Product / UX Implications

  • A "First integration?" onboarding mode that surfaces conceptual context inline — "before you call this endpoint, you'll need an access token; here's why and how."
  • Walkthroughs that explain, not demo — the goal is teaching, with rationale embedded.
  • AI Copilot in "explain mode" — when the user asks "why?" it explains; it does not just answer.
  • Bidirectional concept-to-endpoint links — from "Scopes" to "the endpoints that require this scope," and from each endpoint back to the concept.
  • Error messages with novice-aware hints — "you might be missing X" framed as guidance, not just diagnosis.
Evidence & Confidence Strong · Designer-synthesised

  • SmartBear State of API Documentation: 70% want examples, 51% want errors documented — junior developers over-index on both
  • Sohan et al. (2017) study on usage examples in REST API documentation: inexperienced developers benefit most from worked examples
  • Postman 2024: 44% admit to digging through source code to understand APIs — a documentation gap juniors feel acutely
  • The general literature on bootcamp graduates and onboarding cost in developer-tools UX

The junior developer profile is well-studied across dev-tools UX research, AI-assistant adoption studies, and developer-education literature. Both the tutorial-led behaviour and the AI-as-teacher pattern are documented.

Context

Reza is 41, a Principal Platform Engineer at a large enterprise (financial services, healthcare, or similar regulated industry). Eighteen years of experience. He wrote his first internal SDK in 2012 and has shipped five production APIs at scale. He maintains the internal developer platform that 200+ engineers at his company depend on. Today he is evaluating this API for adoption across multiple business units — his recommendation will determine procurement. He has the OpenAPI spec open in Stoplight, the portal open in another tab, and his team's internal "API evaluation checklist" open in a third.

Goals & Needs

He needs to determine whether the API meets the company's standards for production use. He needs to anticipate integration cost across 5+ downstream teams. He needs to identify gaps that would require him to wrap or extend the SDK with internal logic. He needs the full OpenAPI spec (not just the rendered docs), a coherent error model with HTTP-status semantics, pagination, rate-limit, and bulk semantics that work at scale, webhook reliability documentation (delivery guarantees, signing, replay), data privacy and audit guarantees, SDK source on GitHub, a versioning and deprecation policy he can plan against, and at least published latency p99.

Pain Points

Portals that hide the OpenAPI spec behind nice rendering — he wants the YAML, downloadable, current. SDKs that wrap responses in idiomatic conveniences and lose access to the raw HTTP payload. Marketing pages disguised as technical documentation. AI assistants that summarise away the engineering detail. "Just trust us" answers on rate limits, retry semantics, or webhook delivery.

Behaviours

Downloads the OpenAPI spec immediately. Inspects the SDK source on GitHub before evaluating the docs. Reads the changelog backwards six-plus months to assess freshness and deprecation discipline. Tests rate limits and edge cases in a sandbox before recommending. Compares the API's design choices against his team's standards. Writes a one-page internal review with explicit go/no-go criteria.

Psychographics

  • Motivations Protect the company's engineering quality bar. Avoid the next "we built on this and now we're stuck" disaster. Mentor his platform team.
  • Fears Vendor lock-in costing 7 figures to unwind. Integration problems that surface 18 months later. Roadmap divergence between vendor and his needs.
  • Attitudes Every API is on probation. Documentation tells you 60% of what you need; the rest is in the SDK source and support response time.
  • Risk Tolerance Very low. He absorbs risk on behalf of 200 engineers.
  • Trust Orientation Trusts the spec, the SDK source, the changelog. Distrusts marketing, AI summaries, breathless feature announcements.
  • Technical Confidence Very high. Designs and ships APIs himself.

Decision Criteria

Is the OpenAPI spec clean, current, and complete? Is the error model coherent across endpoints? Does the SDK source pass a code-quality smell test? Is the changelog detailed enough to plan against? Are rate limits, retries, and idempotency explicit, not implied? Does the Copilot answer architect-grade questions or only generate boilerplate?

Cross-spectrum design implications

When the design must serve all five personas, six structural moves emerge as universally beneficial. These are the redesign decisions the spectrum analysis surfaces with the most clarity.

1

Layered information density

Every page must work at multiple registers of expertise — plain-English summary at the top (Aria, Liam), working example in language (Diego), technical detail and edge cases (Maya), spec download and source links (Reza). One page with progressively-revealed depth, not one page per audience.

2

Vocabulary layering in error pages

Error pages answer at three vocabulary levels: "what happened in human terms" (Aria); "what triggered this and how to recover" (Liam, Diego); "response body, retry semantics, idempotency story" (Maya, Reza). The same page serves all three.

3

AI Copilot context awareness

The Copilot's contract must change with context. Plain English for Aria. Explain-mode for Liam. Complete runnable code with OAuth guidance for Diego. Cite-or-refuse for Maya. Spec-grounded architect-grade answers for Reza. A single-mode Copilot satisfies none of them.

4

Multiple entry paths

Aria enters from a Google search of an error. Liam from Quick Start. Diego from the endpoint he needs. Maya from Overview with the spec in mind. Reza from API Spec download. The portal must support every entry point — the homepage is not the start of the journey for four of five personas.

5

Trust signals at every expertise level

Aria trusts plain language and clear escalation. Liam trusts step-by-step + AI-with-reasoning. Diego trusts working examples. Maya trusts citation and error docs. Reza trusts the spec, the source, the changelog. Trust comes from a layered set of signals, each calibrated to its audience.

6

The portal is a multi-audience product

The biggest structural implication is the framing itself. The redesign must explicitly treat the portal as a product serving multiple audiences from one surface, not a "developer document" some non-developers happen to land on. That is a different design brief than most documentation portals operate under.

How extreme personas relate to mode personas

The five extreme personas (Aria → Liam → Diego → Maya → Reza) sit on a single dimension: developer expertise and API sophistication. They stress-test the design at the boundaries.

The three mode personas (Maya, Diego, Sara) sit at comparable expertise levels but in different states of use — evaluating, integrating, troubleshooting. They stress-test the design across the lifecycle.

LensAnchorWhat it surfaces
Mode (v0.1) A working professional developer Lifecycle gaps — where the design fails in evaluation, integration, or troubleshooting
Spectrum (v0.2 / this document) The expertise axis Boundary gaps — where the design fails at the edges of expertise

The two are complementary, not competing. A developer in real life occupies a position on both axes simultaneously — for example, "a junior developer in troubleshooting mode" (intersection of Liam + Sara) is a real moment that neither lens alone captures. Treating the two frameworks as overlays produces a richer test surface for the redesign than either alone.

Sara from v0.1 is not placed on this spectrum because her defining characteristic — debugging-mode behaviour — is orthogonal to expertise. Sara could be Liam-with-an-incident, Diego-with-an-incident, or Maya-with-an-incident. She is best read as a mode any expertise level can enter, not as a position on the expertise spectrum.
