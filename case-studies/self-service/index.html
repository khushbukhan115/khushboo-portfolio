Case study · Self-Service Growth

From sales-gated to self-serve.

A global identity verification provider sold to enterprises through a direct sales process. Startups and small businesses had no equivalent way in, only a lead form that fed the same pipeline. A first attempt at a self-serve path proved people would buy this way, but not that they'd adopt it. I led the redesign that made it a product that could sell itself.

The problem

A front door built for one kind of customer.

Enterprise buyers had a well-worn path in: a sales conversation with someone who understood exactly what they needed. Startups and small businesses had no equivalent. Their only option was a "request a demo" form that fed the same sales pipeline, with no way to try the product first.

The gap was access, not the product. So a first version of a self-serve path was launched to reach that segment directly. It proved the model: smaller companies would sign up and pay this way. What it didn't yet prove was adoption. Most people who signed up never ran the one action that showed them what the product did, and almost none came back to upgrade.

The investigation

One drop-off. Four different problems.

I was not comfortable making product decisions on assumptions, so before designing anything, I rebuilt the customer journey from behavioural analytics, session recordings, and lifecycle data, with input from Sales and Data.

The drop-off didn't look like a single leak once the journey was rebuilt end to end. It looked like a pattern with four separate sources, each needing a different answer.

Business
Pricing had no home for the middle of the market, so upgrades defaulted back to a sales conversation.
Customer
Too many steps stood between clicking in and seeing the product actually work.
Product
Once inside, nothing pulled a new user forward. No milestones, no follow-up, no reason to return.
Organizational
Five functions each optimised their own segment of the journey. None of them owned the whole thing.

The fix followed the same shape: strip the signup down to its minimum before asking anyone to commit; replace a rigid, one-size bundle with a flexible, usage-based model that had a real home for the middle of the market; give the onboarding experience stakes and a lifecycle that responded to what someone actually did, not a calendar; and build a shared way for every function to see the same customer signals, so the fix wouldn't quietly decay back into five disconnected pieces.

The collaboration

Two teams, both right to be cautious.

Finance and Sales both had good reason to push back, and neither was overruled by opinion.

Finance's case was that fixed pricing tiers forecast more predictably than a usage-based model. The evidence showed customers weren't fitting the fixed tiers either, they were rounding themselves up to the least-wrong option. The resolution was bounded flexibility: a usage-based model with limits and predictable billing, close enough to forecastable that Finance could plan against it, close enough to flexible that customers found their actual fit.

Sales' case was that a self-serve path looked like it was cannibalising their pipeline. The resolution there was structural, not persuasive: incentives were rebuilt so growth coming through self-serve counted toward Sales too, which turned the new path into a source of pipeline instead of a competitor to it.

Evidence, not authority, is what brought both teams along.
