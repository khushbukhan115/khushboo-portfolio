Detailed case study · Self-Service Portal

From sales-gated to self-serve.

Shufti is an identity-verification platform that, for most of its history, sold only through sales calls. I redesigned the commercial surface so customers could discover, price, sign up, and verify on their own, turning a sales bottleneck into a revenue engine.

Act I · The problem

A self-service funnel that could not self-serve.

The portal existed. It just did not serve. It read like a sales-led journey wearing a self-service t-shirt: commit as a company, declare your country, then maybe see the product.

How to read this
This is a UX systems case: the research, the funnel decisions, and the commercial architecture behind the redesign. The most consequential choices were not interaction choices, they were decisions about what to design at all.

A prospect landed on the marketing site, scrolled to a pricing page of three cards, clicked "Free sign up," and hit a five-field form asking for company, website, email, country, and personal details before seeing a single screen. Then email verification, a password reset, a login, and a landing page titled "Product Demos." Every stage leaked.

Act II · The data

The upgrade moment lived everywhere except the upgrade page.

First, a clean cohort
The raw export flattered itself. Of 399 records, roughly 31 percent were synthetic QA accounts, all tagged identically and clustered at launch, and about 18 percent more were disposable-email signups. Cleaned, the real cohort was around 275 users. I rebuilt every number on that base, because a funnel you cannot trust is a funnel you cannot fix.

Rather than rely on assumptions, I instrumented the journey: page analytics, session recordings, funnel cohorts, and the first 100 signups. The numbers reframed the problem.

Post-signup, users were exploring with clear buying intent. But the intent showed up on Vendor Comparison and Integrations, not on Plans and Billing, the one page built to convert it.

Post-signup engagement by backoffice section. Strong intent on Vendor Comparison (596) and Integrations (547); only 337 of 1,500 reached Plans and Billing, and Analytics, a power-user signal, saw just 314.

The form was the cliff.

The widest part of the funnel leaked the hardest. A 1.7% completion rate against a 30 to 40% benchmark meant the single highest-leverage surface in the system was the signup form.

Same traffic, two outcomes. At a mid-range SaaS benchmark, the existing 785 monthly visits would yield roughly 235 signups instead of 13, without touching acquisition.

Two leaks, not one.

The Free Forever stage failed in two opposite ways at once. Of the users who never upgraded, about 45 percent logged zero days of use, they signed up and vanished. About 47 percent stayed engaged for a month or more and still never paid. Activation and monetization were separate problems, and a single upgrade nudge would have solved neither.

The tier meant to catch the middle was, in the data, a dead tier. Essentials drew only seven users in thirteen months, and exactly one person ever walked the full Free to Essentials to Enterprise ladder the pricing implied. The intended funnel was not the funnel users actually walked, which is the real argument for rebuilding the middle rather than decorating it.

Two more signals
Traffic was undersupplied, not oversold: an 8.7x gap on organic pricing-page traffic and a 10 to 12.5x gap on AI-originated traffic. And 50 percent of paying customers clustered at a single $2,500 tier, the classic signature of missing tiers, users rounding to the closest-fit plan.
Design the funnel, not the page.
Act III · Strategy

Four bets, one principle.

Every screen had to earn its place by moving a user one step closer to activation, and every activated user needed an obvious, low-friction path to the next plan.

Bet 01

Cut friction aggressively at the top.

Nothing else mattered if people could not get into the product. Reduce signup to minimum viable fields, add one-click Google onboarding, and fix the infrastructure issues (dead clicks, mobile performance) silently taxing every channel.

Bet 02

Rebuild pricing around volume, not packages.

A modular mid-market tier where users configure services and volume on a slider, monthly or yearly, Stripe-native. Close the 11-to-2,499 verification gap with a real product, not a marketing page.

Bet 03

Move the upgrade moment to where intent lives.

Contextual upgrade prompts on the pages where users already signalled intent (Vendor Comparison, Integrations, Analytics). Locked features become interactive upsells, not dead greyed-out buttons.

Bet 04

Instrument the lifecycle.

Stage-by-stage instrumentation and behavioral recording, with a lead-handoff model where signals like repeat pricing visits or stalled activations trigger context-aware outreach.

The tradeoff I defended
Finance and Sales wanted a few fixed SKUs to forecast against. Fixed SKUs caused the $2,500 clustering in the first place. The compromise: bounded flexibility, a sliced volume range with deterministic Stripe billing. Flexible for real users, structured enough to model. I also sequenced the release so each bet's impact could be isolated.

What I designed, and why it mattered.

Six changes, each tied to a specific leak. The first one carried the most weight.

Change 01

Signup simplification.

Stripped to email and password with one-click Google onboarding. The shift was not form optimization, it was commitment shape: the old form asked you to commit as a company before seeing the product; the new one asks you to commit as a person who wants to try it.

Modeled lift 1.7% to 20-30%
Change 02

Pricing in a single viewport, with a calculator.

Four plan cards, a volume calculator that returns a recommended plan and per-check cost, trust signals in the cards, and copy in plain English, partly for humans and partly for the LLMs that now pre-select vendors. Users arrive at signup with a price they picked themselves.

Pre-frames the upgrade path
Change 03

Essentials Plus: a modular, volume-based tier.

Service-first configuration (pick only the checks you need), a per-service volume slider, transparent live price-per-verification, Stripe checkout, mid-cycle top-up, and balance carry-over. The move that closes the mid-market gap and breaks the $2,500 clustering into a spectrum.

Closes the 11 to 2,499 gap
Change 04

Instant onboarding to same-day value.

Google SSO lands the user directly in a "try your first verification" screen against a live sample or their own ID. Sandbox and production become advanced modes, not the default. First value moves from step five-to-seven to step two.

Time-to-value: hours to minutes
Change 05

Move the upgrade moment upstream.

A layered system of contextual nudges, not one banner. Usage-based progressive banners (soft at 60%, sticky at 80%, urgent at 90%), locked features that open into interactive feature previews, and contextual upsells on the high-intent pages where users already are.

Contextual relevance over volume of nudges
Change 06

Service flexibility for horizontal growth.

Inside Essentials Plus, services are additive: add AML at low volume to a Doc-Verification plan with one configuration change and a proration charge, no sales call. Plan changes become a continuous product interaction, every add a near-zero-cost margin increase.

Pilot before the enterprise contract

Act VI · Reflection & what is next

Design is revenue architecture.

  • Design is revenue architecture.The highest-impact decisions were pricing-shape and SKU-architecture choices, and where in the product the upgrade moment happens, not interaction details.
  • The funnel is the product.Pricing, signup, onboarding, and lifecycle are not adjacent surfaces; they are one system with one goal, qualified activated revenue.
  • Intent data beats opinion data.Session recordings and analytics told a more specific story than stakeholder opinion: users did not want more information, they wanted less friction.
  • Contextual beats clever.The best nudges are boring. What makes them work is firing on the right surface, in the right session, at the right threshold. The craft is in placement.
  • LLMs are a traffic channel now.B2B buyers ask an LLM to short-list vendors. A pricing page ambiguous to that model is invisible in the short-list, with direct implications for IA and copy.

The friction inside the team.

Finance wanted fewer SKUs than users needed; I resolved it with bounded flexibility. Engineering could not ship everything in Q1, so I prioritized against the funnel model and deferred the lifecycle layer to Q2. And Sales was uneasy with lead redirection, so I worked with the CRO to reward account executives on expansion revenue from self-serve accounts, not just net-new logos, otherwise every lead-moving decision would have been relitigated weekly.

What I would do next.

  • Ship the full lifecycle nudge system.Locked-feature modals, behavior-triggered re-engagement, and the behavioral alert model for lead handoff.
  • Convert the 596-to-337 leak.Turn Vendor Comparison from an information page into a conversion page; instrument every high-intent page with upgrade CTAs.
  • Make Essentials Plus persona-aware.Recommend a starter configuration from the signup profile, so upgrade becomes a confirmation, not a construction.
  • Rebuild Analytics as the upgrade page.Users who reach Analytics are power users; it is where intent, value, and price converge.
On the figures
Exact revenue remains confidential. The initiative contributed six figures in additional annual recurring revenue through self-serve acquisitions, exceeding the initial plan. The conversion and traffic numbers are modeled targets baked into the financial plan and instrumented across the release waves.
Thesis

The portal was the delivery vehicle. The thing I designed was a funnel that pays for itself.
