Easy to access, not just capable

Observed · Sep 2025 — Mar 2026

For enterprise customers, buying Shufti was easy: they knew what they needed, talked to our account executives, and moved through a familiar sales process. For startups and SMBs, it was the opposite: their only way in was a “Request a Demo” form that fed a sales pipeline, with no way to try the product first.

The gap was access, not the product. So, Shufti launched its pilot self-serve experience to reach the startups and SMBs that competitors were already serving. It proved they would buy this way, but it couldn’t yet deliver the adoption and growth it was built for. This case study is about why, and how I redesigned it into the version that did.

I was not comfortable making product decisions on assumptions.

The context

Shufti: identity verification, in one minute.

Shufti is an identity verification product. It runs the identity and compliance checks (KYC and AML) that companies have to clear before they can onboard a new customer. It’s sold as an API and SaaS product to global enterprises in fintech, banking, gaming, marketplaces, and digital platforms.

That verification is a regulatory requirement, not a formality. A business has to confirm who each customer is and screen them against risk and sanctions lists, and the people who own that work are compliance officers: cautious, regulated, and comparison-shopping across vendors like Sumsub, Persona, Veriff, and Didit.

When Banxy, Natixis’ mobile bank in Algeria, chose Shufti, it was because a prior verification provider had failed 75% of onboarding attempts.

draw

Phase 1, we launched the first pilot of self-serve. When it underperformed, I diagnosed why using the product analytics and customer behaviour data, then led the redesign of pricing, signup, onboarding, activation, and lifecycle.

Phase 1 · The Pilot Self-serve

The first launch worked. It just didn’t work well enough, and we couldn’t yet say why.

The question of why it stalled was mine to answer.

This self-serve experience was launched to open the product to the startups and SMBs that competitors were already serving. It proved the path was real: startups and SMBs signed up on their own, first revenue came in, and self-serve clearly could work at Shufti. What it didn’t yet deliver was adoption. Most companies who signed up never ran a verification, the action that shows what the product does, and almost none came back to upgrade.

Phase 2 · The investigation

The shape of the failure was clear, but everyone had a theory.

I stopped guessing and watched what people actually did.

Instead of relying on one team’s interpretation, I pieced together the customer journey using session recordings, funnel data, sales notes, and lifecycle data. This showed how companies moved from their first visit to their first successful verification.

security

First, the data must earn trust. It came from two places: HubSpot, which tracked who signed up and how far each company got, and Mouseflow, which recorded what people did on the page. The raw export held 399 signup records from the pilot period, a 15-day window. After removing internal QA accounts and disposable email signups, 275 real companies remained, and every figure in this case study is built on this cleaned base.

Why were interested companies never becoming customers?

With a base I could trust, the question got sharper. Not why companies leave in general, but where each one falls out, and what they were trying to do when they did. Following the real companies from the first visit to the first verification, the drop-off stopped looking like a single leak and started looking like a pattern with a few separate sources. The first one showed up before a company had even chosen a plan.

What the investigation surfaced

One drop-off, four problems underneath.

The journey was failing in four places at once. Each explained part of the problem, and each needed a different answer.

Business Plans that could not sell what customers needed, so upgrades fell back to Sales. Customer A funnel that lost people before they ever saw value. Product An experience that gave no reason to come back. Organizational A journey that no single team owned.
01 · The business problem

The pricing had a hole in the middle.

Between 10 free verifications a month and 2,500 a year sat nothing. A company needing 11 to 2,499 verifications — the entire middle of the market — had no plan built for it. Their only route was the Enterprise sales pipeline they did not belong in.

The starvation showed up across the entire Phase 1 launch window — the roughly thirteen months of the first self-serve release, before this redesign began. Essentials, the plan built for that middle, collected 68 form submissions while Enterprise collected 85 — the plan designed for the segment was losing to the plan designed to bypass it. Over those thirteen months, the middle tier had won just 7 customers, and half of all paying customers clustered at exactly the $2,500 price point — customers rounding themselves up to the least-wrong option, not finding a fit.

That was the Phase-1-era picture. At the top of the funnel, in the September 2025 snapshot used throughout the rest of this case study, the mis-routing was starker still. These are two different measurement windows telling the same story, not one funnel: the 68-vs-85 submissions come from the historical Phase 1 pricing-form dataset; the 365-vs-66 CTA-click figure is the September 2025 snapshot.

When I looked at what Free Forever users were actually asking for, the reason was clear. Sales conversations told the story consistently: prospects who should have fit Essentials wanted different verification volumes, different service combinations — Face + Document + AML, single services, custom mixes — and more pricing flexibility than a fixed bundle could offer. Sales reps documented it in HubSpot notes after discovery calls, escalated recurring patterns to Product by email, and repeated it in every Sales–Product handoff meeting. The pattern was impossible to ignore.

The free tier told the same story from below, in that same historical launch-window dataset: of 610 Free Forever signups, 475 were Tier-4 companies and only 8 were Tier-1 — the free plan was attracting the wrong companies while the middle of the market had nowhere to land.

Free Forever wasn’t the limitation

This wasn’t a service-flexibility problem. Free Forever customers were never locked into one fixed bundle — pay-as-you-go let them add AML only, Document Verification only, or any other combination the free allotment didn’t cover, and that flexibility was part of what made the free tier commercially viable. The hole was in volume and plan structure, not service selection: there was still no plan built for a company that needed 11 to 2,499 verifications a year, whatever mix of services it wanted.

The insight

The plan built to serve the middle converted worst. In that same Phase-1-era dataset, Essentials turned about 20% of its form interactions into submissions — against roughly 65–71% for Free Forever and 37% for Enterprise. It did not fit the people who reached it, because it could not sell what they actually needed.

02 · The customer problem

Interested people left before they saw value.

Reach rate rose 31.2% → 45.2% (up 14.0 points) and visitor-to-CTA rate rose 9.7% → 13.7% (up about 4.0 points) after the redesign, while CTA conversion among people who actually reached the comparison held essentially flat — about 31.0% → 30.3%. The honest read: more visitors reached the plan comparison, and the plan mix they selected changed materially — the comparison module itself did not become more persuasive. 04 · The organizational problem

Everyone owned a piece. No one owned the journey.

Underneath all of it was a structural problem. Each function optimized its own stretch of the funnel, and did it well. Marketing owned traffic, Sales owned leads, Finance owned margins, Engineering owned delivery, Customer Success owned tickets. But the journey a customer travels runs across all of them, and no one owned that whole. The gaps between functions were exactly the gaps where customers fell out.

The insight

That is why the problem stayed invisible. Every team could see its own segment. No one was measuring the space between them.

Naming these four problems changed the brief. The project stopped being a self-service cleanup and became a redesign of how the company sells.

The decisions

Instead of redesigning onboarding, I redesigned how customers became customers.

The project expanded out of UX into product strategy, pricing, lifecycle, growth, and commercial operations. It came down to four decisions — each one an evidence claim, a bet, and a cost we accepted with open eyes.

Decision 01 Reduce commitment before value.

Evidence. Session recordings showed users abandoning the signup form after 1–2 of its 5 required fields, and the activation chain ran 8 steps from click to product — through a popup, an inbox detour, and a 12-character password gate — while prospects were still comparison-shopping.

Decision. Strip signup to its minimum. Location and Website fields removed, mandatory fields cut, corporate Google sign-in added, company information moved to progressive profiling inside the product. The activation chain fell from 8 steps to 3.

Trade-off accepted. We gave up the enrichment data Sales and Marketing used to capture at signup — location, company website, phone — fields that used to feed lead scoring before anyone touched the product. Qualification moved from what a form said to what a user actually did, and it held up: by the time the redesign was live, only 31.7% of small leads still needed a human to qualify them, versus 100% before.

Decision 02 Replace the pricing with a growth model.

Evidence. Essentials — the $2,495 annual bundle — was our own first answer to the middle market, and we measured it honestly. HubSpot sales notes and every Sales–Product handoff said the same thing: buyers wanted their own combinations of services and volumes, not our bundle.

Decision. We killed the annual bundle. In its place: usage-based pricing — core services bundled, every additional service selectable individually with transparent per-service pricing, the price updating live as services are added, and checkout completing in self-serve with no sales call. The 11-to-2,499 middle now had two honest homes: Free + Pay-as-you-go below, configurable Essential above.

Trade-off accepted. Finance argued — correctly — that fixed tiers forecast better than usage curves. The compromise was bounded flexibility: sliders with limits, deterministic Stripe billing, no unlimited configurations. Forecastability gave some ground to fit; it did not surrender to it. It shipped as Essential.

The redesign did not make direct Essential purchases more efficient — it made them less necessary. Buy-Now clicks grew 66 → 590 and direct purchases grew in volume, 5 → 21, but the purchase rate fell: click-to-purchase 7.6% → 3.6%; checkout-to-purchase 8.3% → 3.9%. That is not a contradiction — most new paid conversion was designed to arrive through the Free → Essential upgrade route instead (27/month, see Proof below), because trial-first was the whole point of killing the bundle. But it is a real trade-off, stated here rather than left for a screener to discover unaided.

Decision 03 Turn onboarding into behavioural design.

Evidence. The activated users toured the back office — comparison pages, integrations, settings — but the sandbox never asked them to do anything, never expired, and never followed up. One welcome email, then silence.

Decision. Give the sandbox stakes and the lifecycle a nervous system. Available checks and remaining time made visible in the sandbox; activation milestones defined; the single welcome email replaced by 8 behaviour-triggered workflows — nurture, activation nudges, usage triggers, upgrade moments, re-engagement — each keyed to what a user actually did, not to a calendar.

Trade-off accepted. Urgency risks pressure, and triggered email risks fatigue. We bounded both: triggers fire on behaviour, not schedules, and every nudge has a defined exit condition.

Decision 04 Design the growth system, and give the journey an owner.

Evidence. Problem 04: five functions, five optimized segments, no owner of the whole. Any redesign that shipped screens without shipping ownership would decay back to the same gaps.

Decision. The biggest deliverable was not a screen — it was a behavioural operating system. I led this work and aligned the departments behind it: HubSpot lifecycle data came through Sales, Mouseflow behavioural analysis was my own UX work, and product-usage analytics came from Engineering, Data, and ML. Working across Product, Engineering, Marketing, Sales, and Customer Success, I designed HubSpot workflows driven by real product events — signup, first verification, inactivity, power use, quota consumption, upgrade signals, sales notifications. That behavioural-signal system became the shared ownership mechanism: for the first time, every function watched the same signals from the same journey. The solution was argued, aligned, and operationalised across teams, not only designed.

Trade-off accepted. Design now owned a system with an operating cost — signal definitions, trigger maintenance, cross-team governance — not a set of screens it could hand off and leave.

How it was argued, not just drawn

Finance · pricing shape

Fixed tiers forecast better; my evidence said the $2,500 clustering was customers rounding to the least-wrong option.

We landed on bounded flexibility — sliders, deterministic Stripe billing.

Engineering · release strategy

The full roadmap was unaffordable. We prioritised by expected impact per unit of build.

Q1 — signup simplification, dead-click fixes, mobile, the new pricing page, Essential. Q2 — the lifecycle layer, locked-feature modals, MDR triggers.

Sales · the incentive model

To account executives, self-serve looked like stolen deals.

The CRO re-cut incentives toward expansion revenue from self-serve accounts — self-serve became Sales’ pipeline instead of their competitor.

And one engineering ticket

Session recordings showed repeat clicks on images users expected to be buttons — a rendering bug, not a design flaw.

The dead-click rate fell 6.2% → 1.1% after the fix. The most valuable design research sometimes files an engineering ticket.

Before, 5.5× more interactions routed to Enterprise’s sales-led CTAs than to Essential (365 vs 66); after, Essential drew 2.3× the interactions of Enterprise (590 vs 256), and Enterprise interactions fell 29.9% despite 46% more traffic overall. This is CTA-click counts, not unique visitors or a survey of intent — share of pricing-page CTA interactions, read as interpretation, not measurement.

The full before/after acquisition picture: 10,418 → 15,247 pricing visitors (+46.4%); 3,251 (31.2%) → 6,890 (45.2%) reached the plan comparison; 1,009 (9.7%) → 2,090 (13.7%) clicked any CTA. Read with the customer section’s interpretation: exposure to value grew substantially; the comparison block’s own conversion efficiency, once reached, was essentially flat (about 31.0% → 30.3%). What changed materially was which plan people chose once they got there.

What this adds up to

Essential’s own direct-purchase rate is a companion story worth holding alongside the CTA shift. Buy Now clicks grew 66 → 590 and reached checkout 60 → 545, but the purchase rate fell — click-to-purchase 7.6% → 3.6%, checkout-to-purchase 8.3% → 3.9% — because most new paid conversion arrived through the Free → Essential upgrade route instead, by design, not by failure. Enterprise moved the other way: submitted-to-qualified rose 62.2% → 76.1%, and sales-qualified leads rose 61 → 67 despite fewer Contact Sales clicks (121 → 104) — fewer, better-fit conversations, exactly the “reduce sales dependency” goal. Instead of disappearing after the first login, users kept moving through the product lifecycle, and the organisation started making decisions from customer behaviour instead of assumptions.

The real outcome

Shufti stopped thinking like a company that sold software through Sales. It started building a product that could sell itself.

What this project changed about me

Great UX removes friction. Great product design changes behaviour.

Before this project, I believed great UX was about removing friction. After it, I understood that great product design changes behaviour. The screens mattered. The research mattered. The flows mattered. But none of them created business impact on their own.

The impact came from finding the right problem, aligning six departments around a shared vision, and redesigning the commercial system rather than individual interfaces.

And not everything moved. Activation improved least of everything we touched — visibility and urgency helped, but the sandbox needed a stronger first-success design than we shipped in Q1. Essential’s direct-purchase rate is the other honest miss — it fell even as the plan attracted far more consideration, because conversion was designed to arrive through upgrades instead. Attribution is shared, too: traffic growth belongs partly to Marketing’s work; what the redesign owns is conversion and the capture of the middle market.

That is the lesson I have carried into every product since: systems change behaviour; screens just host it.
