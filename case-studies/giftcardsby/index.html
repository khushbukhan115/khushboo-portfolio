GiftCardsby.com · Merit Incentives · 2021–2022

Productising enterprise gifting for the self-serve market

From high-touch enterprise delivery to self-serve SaaS

Launched 9 Aug 2022 Pay-as-you-go talabat partnership Still live · 2026

Merit Incentives could already run digital gifting at scale — issuance, payments, redemption, fulfilment, and a network of thousands of partner brands. But it could only sell that capability the enterprise way: custom-built, one high-touch programme at a time. GiftCardsby.com changed the unit of delivery — turning capability Merit already owned into a product a business could run itself, from an independent shop up to a global brand.

I joined as Lead UX/UI Designer and led the MVP experience: the path a merchant takes from signing up to a live, sellable, branded gift card, and the purchase and redemption experiences around it.

The real problem was never "design a gift-card tool." It was to decide the smallest set of decisions a merchant should ever have to make, and let the platform carry the rest — a full commercial system running out of sight.

The opportunity

A market the enterprise model couldn't reach

For a small business, a gift card is not decoration. It is prepaid revenue before a product is redeemed, a way to reach a new customer through the person who receives the gift, and a reason for people to come back. Merit cites industry figures that the majority of gift-card shoppers spend beyond the value of the card, with the uplift especially pronounced in this region.

~60% spend beyond card value
Up to 450% of gifted value in MENA
Industry-cited · Gulf Business, Aug 2022

The barrier was never demand. It was infrastructure. Setting up digital gifting traditionally meant software, card generation, payment processing, redemption rules, security, customer communication, reporting, and ongoing administration. For an independent business, the cost and effort outweighed the opportunity, so most simply went without.

That is the gap GiftCardsby.com was built to close — and closing it for the long tail of small merchants, not just for enterprises, was the point. The product had to remove the infrastructure problem, not add another marketing tool.

Why Merit could build it

Productising is only smart when you already own the hard part

Merit did. The issuance engine, payment logic, redemption infrastructure, fulfilment, and merchant relationships were built and paid for by the enterprise business. The self-serve product did not have to rebuild any of it; it had to expose a thin, safe slice of it to a new kind of user.

That is the economic logic of the whole project. Because the platform was reused rather than rebuilt, the marginal cost of onboarding one more small merchant was low — exactly what makes a self-serve SaaS model work where high-touch enterprise delivery does not. My job was to design the surface that made that reuse feel like a product rather than a stripped-down enterprise console.

The design thesis

Subtraction, not shrinking

The wrong way to build this would have been to take the enterprise product and cram every capability into a smaller interface. That reproduces the complexity; it just makes it harder to read.

The design problem was the opposite: identify the minimum set of decisions a merchant genuinely has to make to create a gift card that is trustworthy, sellable, and redeemable, and let the platform absorb everything else. Every screen was a question of subtraction — what can this person not have to think about, without losing what they need to trust the result?

The experience I designed

Onboarding that opens a door, not a project

A merchant creates an account with an email and essential business details, then verifies by email to activate. Onboarding asks only for what it needs to open the account; operational depth waits until it is relevant. The first impression is "I can do this," not "this is a system to learn."

Merchant workspace / dashboard

After activation, the merchant lands in a dashboard built as the operating home of their programme: create a card, edit drafts, review what is published, watch activity, reach settings. It is where you run the thing, not just where designs are stored.

The hero screen

A card creator organised around three real decisions

Card creation is organised around three kinds of decision, and nothing else. A live preview sits alongside, so the merchant can judge the finished card — design, value, message, and terms together — before committing to publish.

Brand

Logo, identity, artwork, styling — the merchant owns presentation.

Commercial

Value and denominations, currency, validity and expiry, where and on what the card applies.

Customer-facing

Card name, occasion, message, recipient information, redemption instructions.

Publish, sell, deliver, redeem

The card publishes to the merchant's own branded storefront. A customer picks a design and value, pays, and sends the card to a recipient, who receives it with its value, the merchant's identity, and clear instructions to redeem. The platform holds the code and the transaction state; the merchant validates redemption through the supported flow.

Three tensions defined the hard part of the work.

visibility

Simplicity vs. transparency

Value, validity, where a card works, redemption conditions, and expiry had to stay visible and legible through purchase and redemption, even as everything operational disappeared. Hide the infrastructure, never the terms of the deal.

tune

Freedom vs. control

Merchants needed real freedom to design for Ramadan and Eid, Christmas and New Year, National Day, weddings, corporate thank-yous. The card creator gave them ownership of presentation while the platform kept the structural fields it needs to issue, price, and track the card.

dashboard

Designing a card vs. running a programme

Making an attractive card and operating a gift-card programme are related but different jobs. I kept them structurally separate: content in the card creator, everything commercial and operational in the workspace around it.

The rule I held

Hide the infrastructure, never the terms of the deal. A gift card is a promise between three people, and each has to be able to act on it with confidence.

What's actually proven

What is verifiable is the design and the proposition: the product shipped, priced pay-as-you-go, launched with a named regional partner (talabat), and still runs on that model today. What I cannot show is adoption or revenue for the product — that data is not mine to present, and Merit's company-level growth reflects its whole business, not this MVP. The honest claim is that the model created the potential for returns on all three sides, and that the proposition has remained commercially relevant for years.

My MVP contribution was the merchant-facing experience. The decision to productise, the commercial model, the underlying platform, and the go-to-market were owned by Merit's product, engineering, and business teams. I designed how it felt to use; they decided what it would be and how it would sell.

What I took from it

A subtraction problem of a different order

Before this project, I thought good UX was mostly about removing friction. GiftCardsby.com taught me that productising an enterprise system is a subtraction problem of a different order: the craft is deciding what a person should never have to see, while protecting the few things they need to trust the result.

If I were building it again, I would instrument the merchant journey from the first day — sign-up, first card, first sale, redemption — so a productisation bet this size could be proven with numbers, not only argued well.

“Design the system, measure the behaviour, and let the screens serve both.”
