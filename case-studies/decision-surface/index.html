Act I · The Constraint

A verification platform
that couldn't be used.

Veridian is an identity verification platform used by banks, fintechs, and regulated businesses across 230+ countries to run KYC, AML, KYB, and biometric checks.

For most of its history, getting onto the platform meant one thing: talking to a salesperson. Every prospect, from a two-person fintech to an enterprise running a million checks, went through the same high-touch funnel.

The market shifted. Startups, SMBs, and developers wanted to evaluate, test, and activate the same day. the way they do with Stripe or Twilio. Instead, they hit a contact form, waited for a sales rep, and churned before the first call.

The platform existed. The decision environment (the surfaces that let a user evaluate, commit, and escalate) did not.

The system asked users to declare intent
before it had given them any reason to trust it.
Act II · The Architecture

KYC is not a form.
It is a decision pipeline.

The first design move was refusing to treat this as a flow-length problem.

KYC and KYB verification is a pipeline of sequential decisions. Each step requires a different actor, a different confidence threshold, and a different response to uncertainty. A user selecting document type is making a claim. The system evaluating that document is making an inference. A compliance officer reviewing a flagged verification is making a judgment.

The existing interface had been designed for one actor: the sales rep. explaining the product to someone who had already agreed to buy. That structure had no place for self-directed evaluation, no response to uncertainty, and no escalation path that the user could see.

Intent to buy was present. It was in the wrong place.

Act III · The Decision Environment

Each signal changes
what the system does next.

Redesigning the platform meant redesigning how the system reads user state and responds in context. Not a set of pages. A decision loop.

Every surface a user touches is a signal. Every signal has an appropriate system response. If the system can't read the signal, it can't respond correctly.

Decision architecture, not flow design
This loop is structurally identical to the decision pipeline in any high-stakes procedural system: observe the actor's state, infer their confidence and intent, respond proportionally. The specific domain is identity verification. The design contract generalizes: if the system cannot observe state, it cannot respond correctly. If it cannot respond correctly, complexity is the user's problem, not the system's.
Act IV · AI in the System

AI was not added.
It was designed in from the start.

Two distinct roles for AI: one inside the product, one inside the design process. Both were deliberate, not incidental.

In-product · Document inference

The system reads what the user uploads. It doesn't ask what they already know.

Country and document type had been a manual selection step. Users declared information the system could derive from the document itself. Removing the selection screen removed a class of mismatch errors where user declaration diverged from actual upload. The system now infers both at capture time.

In-product · LLM-parseable design

The pricing page was written for two audiences: the human buyer and the AI they use to shortlist.

B2B buyers increasingly ask Claude or ChatGPT to shortlist vendors. If your pricing page is ambiguous to that model, you're absent from the shortlist. Structured copy, plain-English plan names, and schema-ready metadata were designed in. Modeled outcome: 10–12.5× lift in AI-originated inbound traffic.

In-process · Behavioral analysis

Mouseflow recordings surfaced patterns. Claude helped turn 100 session recordings into a prioritized friction map in hours, not days.

The dead-click finding — users clicking non-interactive card regions expecting navigation, came from LLM-assisted session analysis. A pattern that would have taken a week of manual review was surfaced in a single afternoon. The root cause was a rendering bug, not a design problem. That distinction changed the sprint scope.

In-process · Pricing scenario modeling

Essentials Plus was modeled as a decision tree before a single Figma frame was opened.

Used Claude to simulate how users with different volume and service configurations would navigate the new pricing architecture. The goal was to find the edge cases before engineering did: what happens when a user wants AML at 2,500/mo but Face at 20,000/mo? The configurator was designed around the edge cases that surfaced, not the median path.

Agentic · HubSpot lead intelligence

Every inbound MQL was scored for conversion priority and deal scope before a human touched it.

Built an agent that pulls MQL signals from HubSpot, scores each lead against conversion likelihood and estimated revenue scope, and surfaces a prioritised queue for the sales team. When an assigned MQL goes cold, the agent does not wait: it detects non-response, infers friction as the likely cause, adjusts the lead's priority, and triggers a re-sequence automatically. Qualification became a system output, not a manual judgment call.

Agentic · Mouseflow funnel automation

Funnel creation and drop-off analysis became a single command, not a configuration session.

Built an agent that programmatically generates Mouseflow funnels from product event data and analyses them without manual setup. What previously required hand-defining funnels, waiting for session volume to accumulate, and interpreting reports one by one was compressed into an automated pipeline. Bottleneck identification, drop-off sequencing, and session tagging now run continuously. Behavioral insight went from a periodic research task to ambient infrastructure.

I didn't just use AI in the design process.
I built the agents that ran it.

The combination of behavioral analysis, LLM-assisted scenario modeling, automated funnel generation, and lead intelligence agents compressed the research-to-spec timeline from weeks to days, and surfaced decision points that a purely manual process would have missed.

Act V · System Rewrites

Six surfaces redesigned.
One principle applied to all.

The principle: every surface must respond to the user's current state, not assume a linear journey through it.

Rewrite 01

Commitment was moved downstream.

The original signup form asked users to declare their company, website, and country before seeing the product. The redesign stripped entry to email + password, with Google SSO as primary path. Company context is gathered progressively, inside the product, once value has been demonstrated.

This is not a form-length decision. It is a commitment-shape decision: when in the relationship should the system ask for what.

Shift · pre-value commitment → post-value context gathering
Rewrite 02

Pricing became a configurator, not a table.

A volume calculator replaced static plan cards. Users input expected verification count; the system returns the recommended plan with exact per-check cost. The mid-market gap (11 to 2,499 verifications per year) was closed with Essentials Plus: service-first configuration, per-service volume sliders, Stripe-native checkout, no sales touch.

The $2,500 clustering pattern (50% of paying customers at one tier because no other tier fit) was a pricing architecture problem, not a conversion problem. The calculator surfaced it. The new architecture resolved it.

Shift · plan selection → volume-configured self-serve
Rewrite 03

Upgrade moments moved to where intent lived.

Plans & Billing had 22.5% of dashboard traffic. Vendor Comparison had 596 users actively comparing. The upgrade surface was in the wrong place. Contextual upgrade prompts were added to Vendor Comparison, Integrations, and Analytics: the three surfaces where buying intent was already signalling.

Locked modules became interactive previews, not dead grey states. Clicking a locked feature now opens a decision surface: what it does, what it costs to unlock, one CTA.

Shift · centralized upgrade page → contextual conversion
Rewrite 04

Services became additive, not a plan upgrade.

Adding AML to a Face + Doc plan previously required moving to a higher tier. The redesign made services independent: a user on Doc Verification at 5,000/mo can add AML at 2,500/mo with a single configuration change and a proration charge. No sales call. No renegotiation.

This turned plan expansion from a discrete commercial event into a continuous product interaction.

Shift · plan renegotiation → service composition
Rewrite 05

Analytics was designed as the upgrade surface, not a reporting page.

314 of 1,500 dashboard users ever reached Analytics. Those who did were almost certainly power users. High usage, high value, and high openness to expansion, but nothing on the Analytics page converted that intent.

Usage data, locked capability previews, and upgrade CTAs were woven into the Analytics dashboard. Where behavior and price converge, the upgrade moment belongs.

Shift · reporting page → conversion surface
Rewrite 06

The failure mode was instrumented.

Before this project, there was no metric for users who stalled in the verification flow. Completion was tracked. Stalling was not. A new metric was introduced: recovery rate: of users who show a signal of being stuck, the percentage who re-enter and complete.

The team did not have this number before. Once it existed, stuck users became a trackable cohort, not an unexplained drop-off.

Shift · raw completion → recovery rate as first-class metric

Act VII · Transfer

Where this thinking applies
beyond verification.

Identity verification is a specific domain. The decision-architecture problems it surfaces are not.

Adjudication platforms

Claim submission through decision output

Any platform where a claim is made, evidence is evaluated, and a decision is rendered carries the same architecture: observe actor state, infer confidence, respond proportionally. The interface must handle the full pipeline, from initial submission through escalation, review, and output, without making complexity the user's problem.

AI-assisted workflows

When the system's confidence is partial

Designing for uncertain model output is the same problem as designing for uncertain user state. The system must decide when to surface its confidence, when to intervene, and when to escalate to human judgment. Recovery design and confidence design are the same discipline.

High-trust, regulated products

When the stakes are institutional

KYC and KYB are institutional decisions. Every design choice either builds or erodes the trust placed in the system by the user, the regulator, and the downstream consumer of the decision. That constraint does not change by domain. It is the constant in every high-stakes decision surface.

In hindsight

I didn't redesign a platform.
I designed the environment in which decisions get made.
