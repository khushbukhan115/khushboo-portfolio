№ 05How I lead design

Operating principles. Not aspirations.

01

Design owns the problem definition.

Every brief gets argued before it gets built. We rewrite what we were asked to do, in writing, before anyone opens Figma. Briefs that survive the argument ship faster. Briefs that do not survive save the team a quarter.

02

The interface is the last layer.

My designers read the API, sit with engineering, understand the data model, and shadow the operator. We do not start in Figma. We start where the system actually lives. Designers who cannot read an API do not work on regulated flows on my team.

03

Compliance and risk are co-authors.

The design that ignores compliance is the design that ships late. Legal, compliance, and risk join at the structure phase, not the polish phase. Treating them as gatekeepers is a junior mistake that costs senior teams a quarter.

04

Decisions get written down. Including the rejected ones.

Every consequential design decision in my org has a one-page record: the choice, the rejected alternatives, the reasoning, the tradeoff. Memory is not a deliverable.

05

Junior designers ship. Senior designers structure.

Juniors get ownership early on small surfaces. Seniors and I take the high-ambiguity, cross-team work where the answer is the structure, not the screen. The team scales because structural work is the only work that scales designers.

I do not run design as a service team. I run a discipline that owns parts of the product no one else can.

№ 06Designing AI in regulated systems

Most teams put AI in the wrong layer.

They put it in front of the user when it belongs behind the operator. They automate decisions that should require an override. Both choices ship faster and break later.

Where AI belongs

Augmenting an operator who already has authority to decide. Pre-classifying incoming work to route it faster. Surfacing signals the operator would miss. Drafting language a human will edit. Explaining why a model made a call so a human can disagree with it.

Where AI is a liability

Final-decision automation in flows that affect access to financial services, identity, or legal status. High-confidence presentation of output that is statistically often wrong. Free-text in regulated fields where every word can be quoted in an audit. Hidden AI: model output dressed up as a rules-based decision so the operator cannot challenge it.

Three questions before any AI surface ships
01
Who is accountable when the model is wrong?
If the answer is "no one," the design is wrong, not the model.
02
Where does the override live, and how cheap is it to use?
An override that requires senior approval is not an override.
03
Does the design make it easy or hard to spot a wrong output?
If the model's confidence and signals are not visible, the human is rubber-stamping.

The AI feature is not the product. The surface around it is. Regulated companies do not need a model. They need a way to use one without losing their license.
